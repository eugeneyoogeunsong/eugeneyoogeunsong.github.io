People occasionally ask why my CV reads like four careers at once: cosmology, then black holes, then machine learning for medical physics, then neutrinos, with quantum computing and financial markets alongside. It is a fair question, and the honest answer is short: I have only ever worked on **one problem**.

The problem is this. You have noisy, incomplete data from a process you understand only partially, and you want a statement about the world that is not merely precise but **calibrated**: one whose error bar means what it claims to mean. A small uncertainty is easy to manufacture and worth nothing by itself; what matters is whether the number is honest about how wrong it might be, because somebody is going to act on it. A cardiologist decides whether to put in a stent. A neutrino collaboration decides whether it has seen matter and antimatter behave differently. Neither decision cares how impressive your resolution looked in isolation.

The tour, with the vocabulary stripped out:

- [Early-universe cosmology](/projects/10_cosmology/): does a universe that starts out lopsided smooth itself out, and how fast?
- [Black hole magnetospheres](/projects/9_gamma/): can a spinning black hole act as a particle accelerator, and would we see the gamma rays?
- [Cardiology](/projects/3_atripinn/): from a grid of electrodes on a fibrillating heart, where is the electrical wave, and how sure are we?
- [Neutrinos](/projects/1_dune/): when a detector near the beam and one 1,300 km away disagree, how much is physics and how much is the instrument?
- [Coronary blood flow](/projects/5_singularity/): from a CT scan alone, how badly does a narrowed artery restrict the flow?
- [Markets](/projects/4_quant/): when the rules of a market change, how do you notice before you have paid for not noticing?

Six questions, one shape: a governing equation you trust, a measurement you do not entirely, and a decision waiting at the end. What varies is only which part you are least sure of.

The vocabulary gives the game away. In the blood-flow problem, accuracy is not limited by how fast the solver runs; it is limited by the outlet boundary conditions, i.e., what you assume about the smaller vessels downstream that the scan cannot see. Make a thousand-fold faster solver and you converge on the wrong answer sooner. In markets, the boundary conditions are impact, inventory, liquidity and latency (how much your own trading moves the price, and how quickly you can get out), and a strategy specified without them has not been specified. Same phrase in two unrelated fields, and not by coincidence: in both, it names the place where a clean problem meets a world you only partly observe.

Once you see this, moving between fields stops feeling like starting over and starts feeling like carrying the same toolkit into a new room. The furniture is unfamiliar; the tools are not.

So that is what this blog is for: the work, from inside it. What I am reading; what broke this week; and, now and then, the thing that finally worked. Expect few posts, and real ones, in English, since most of my [network](https://www.linkedin.com/in/yoogeunsong) is in Europe or the USA and that is the one language that reaches all of it.
